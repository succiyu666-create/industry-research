# Six-Question Industry Framework

Use this reference for standard and deep industry analysis. The questions form one system: the boundary defines the object; money shows transactions; value explains why transactions exist; profit reveals power; risk reveals loss allocation; change tests whether the structure can persist.

## 1. Boundary — What is the industry?

Determine:

- The underlying customer job or need.
- Payer, buyer, user, beneficiary, and influencer when they differ.
- Included products, services, and activities.
- Explicit exclusions, substitutes, complements, and adjacent industries.
- Useful segmentations by product, customer, use case, technology, business model, or geography.
- The unit of analysis and the correct denominator for market sizing.

Minimum output: a one-sentence definition, inclusion/exclusion list, taxonomy, and boundary caveats.

Boundary errors contaminate every later conclusion. Do not combine estimates whose definitions differ merely because they share an industry label.

## 2. Money — Who funds the system?

Trace distinct sources:

- Operating money: product sales, subscriptions, usage, commissions, services, ads, licensing, or transaction fees.
- Capital: equity, debt, strategic investment, and internal group funding.
- Public support: subsidies, procurement, tax benefits, grants, or policy lending.
- Cross-subsidy: profits from another product, business unit, or participant.

Then trace allocation to materials, infrastructure, R&D, labor, production, delivery, acquisition, channel, compliance, financing cost, taxes, and owners.

Ask:

- Who is the ultimate payer?
- Are payer, user, and beneficiary different?
- Who controls billing, settlement, payment terms, and working capital?
- Which actors depend on external finance rather than operating cash flow?
- Where does cash accumulate, and where is it structurally consumed?

Minimum output: funding-source table, cash-flow path, and capital-intensity profile.

## 3. Value — Why does the industry exist?

For each link in the chain identify:

- Inputs and required capabilities.
- The transformation performed.
- The output and its downstream customer.
- Value created through lower cost, higher revenue, speed, convenience, quality, access, risk reduction, data, trust, compliance, or coordination.
- Product, service, information, data, and trust flows.
- Replaceability, standardization potential, and dependency.

Test each link by asking what breaks if it disappears. Separate value creation from historical intermediation and regulatory necessity.

Minimum output: value-chain diagram plus an `input → capability → output → beneficiary → replaceability` table.

## 4. Profit and power — Who captures value?

Separate revenue pools, gross-profit pools, operating-profit pools, free cash flow, and return on invested capital. Consider capital, inventory, labor, and time required to earn each return.

Locate durable power from:

- Scarcity or control of a critical resource.
- Technology, patents, standards, licenses, or regulatory access.
- Scale, network, learning, or data effects.
- Distribution, customer ownership, brand, trust, or switching costs.
- Control of billing, discovery, interoperability, or transaction rules.

Ask whether current profit is structural or temporarily caused by scarcity, subsidy, cycle, regulation, or accounting presentation. Group competitors by strategic approach, not merely by size.

Minimum output: revenue-versus-profit pool, power map, strategic groups, and likely profit-pool migration.

## 5. Risk — Who ultimately bears loss?

Consider demand, price, supply, inventory, technology, substitution, regulation, operations, quality, financial, liquidity, credit, concentration, data, security, reputation, geopolitical, and systemic risks.

For each material risk trace:

`source → trigger → first bearer → transmission path → transfer mechanism → ultimate bearer → economic loss`

Transfer mechanisms may include pricing, contract terms, prepayment, minimum commitments, inventory buyouts, outsourcing, insurance, derivatives, collateral, limited liability, bankruptcy, or public support.

Ask:

- Does the risk bearer receive adequate compensation?
- Who enjoys upside while shifting downside?
- Which actor has the least ability to pass risk onward?
- Which risk could propagate through the system?

Minimum output: risk-allocation matrix, transmission map, and risk-return mismatches.

## 6. Change — What could reconfigure the system?

Investigate changes in demand, supply, technology, policy, capital, competition, costs, demographics, social trust, trade, and geopolitics.

Do not present a trend list. Build causal chains:

`driver → first affected variable → actor response → money/value/risk-flow change → industry structure → profit migration → winners/losers → leading indicator`

Assess lifecycle stage and distinguish structural change from cyclical fluctuation or a one-off event. Use scenarios when outcomes depend on uncertain conditions. Give each scenario explicit assumptions, invalidation conditions, winners, losers, profit migration, risk migration, and leading indicators.

Minimum output: three to five causal chains, scenario conditions where useful, and a monitoring dashboard.

## Integrated diagnosis

After completing the six questions, answer:

- Which three nodes create the most indispensable value?
- Which three nodes hold the most power?
- Are value creation and profit capture aligned?
- Who bears the most risk, and who transfers risk most effectively?
- Which actors combine high profit, low capital intensity, and strong risk transfer?
- Which actors combine low profit, high capital intensity, and weak risk transfer?
- What assumption holds the current structure together?
- Which single variable is most capable of changing the whole system?
