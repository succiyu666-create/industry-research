# Industry Six Questions

Build a causal model of how an industry works. Do not substitute a long report, a company list, or a collection of market-size figures for understanding.

The model must answer:

1. **Boundary** — What is the industry, and what is outside it?
2. **Money** — Who pays, where does funding enter, and where does cash go?
3. **Value** — Where is value created, transformed, and delivered?
4. **Profit and power** — Who captures profit, and what gives them durable bargaining power?
5. **Risk** — What can go wrong, how does risk travel, and who ultimately bears the loss?
6. **Change** — What forces could alter the other five parts of the model?

## Start with a research brief

Establish the decision the research must support, geographic and time scope, focal customer or segment, and explicit exclusions. If the user does not provide them, make minimal assumptions and label them. Resolve material boundary ambiguity before calculating market size or naming competitors.

Choose a depth proportional to the request:

- **Quick map** — explain the six questions, major actors, and the most important unknowns.
- **Standard analysis** — add sourced numbers, flow maps, profit and risk allocation, and change mechanisms.
- **Deep research** — add claim-level evidence tracking, contradiction analysis, scenarios, and a monitoring system.

## Build the model

Read [references/framework.md](references/framework.md) before a standard or deep analysis. Use it to investigate all six questions without treating them as isolated chapters.

Maintain these distinctions:

- Money flow is not value flow.
- Revenue passing through an actor is not profit captured by that actor.
- Value creation is not necessarily value capture.
- The first party exposed to a risk may not be its ultimate bearer.
- A trend is not a change mechanism until the causal path is explained.

Map relationships among actors. A useful map names who supplies whom, who pays whom, who controls the transaction, who can switch, and who absorbs failure. A logo wall is not an industry map.

## Research and evidence

For current, disputed, numerical, regulatory, or decision-critical claims, retrieve current evidence rather than relying on memory. Read [references/evidence-and-quality.md](references/evidence-and-quality.md) when external research is required.

For each major conclusion, preserve:

`conclusion → evidence → mechanism → implication → uncertainty`

Label facts, inferences, hypotheses, forecasts, and attributed opinions. Attach year, geography, unit, and definition to important numbers. Keep incompatible market estimates separate and explain the difference in scope or method.

## Synthesize causally

Connect the six questions instead of merely filling headings. A strong synthesis explains, for example:

`customer need → payment source → value chain → bottleneck power → profit capture → risk transfer → structural change`

For each major change driver, show:

`driver → first affected variable → actor response → changes to money/value/risk flows → structural result → winners and losers → observable indicator`

## Deliver the answer

Match the format and length to the user's purpose. For a reusable full report, read [references/output-templates.md](references/output-templates.md). Unless the user requests another form, include:

1. An executive model in one page or less.
2. The six-question analysis.
3. At least one integrated actor table covering value, revenue, profit, power, capital intensity, risk, and risk-transfer ability.
4. The most important causal chains and leading indicators.
5. Evidence gaps, unresolved contradictions, and the next questions to verify.

Prefer diagrams and tables only when they clarify relationships. Do not create false precision. If the evidence is insufficient, deliver a partial model with named gaps instead of a polished but unsupported conclusion.
